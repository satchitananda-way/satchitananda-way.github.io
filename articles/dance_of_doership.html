The Dance of Doership: A Dialogue

Ramu: Somu, I'm telling you—without discipline, nothing happens. Look at your house! Before you can invite a guest, the front step must be cleaned spotless. The shrine room must be swept, the lamp must be lit with care. Every morning, I wake at 5 AM, I do my practices, I maintain my space. This is how transformation happens—through consistent, dedicated effort.
Somu: Ramu, Ramu... you're making life unnecessarily difficult. Tell me something—when you want to remove darkness from a room, do you fight with the darkness? Do you take a stick and battle the shadows? No! You simply switch on the light, and the darkness vanishes. When one thing is naturally accomplished, the next follows. Understanding is the light switch. Why all this exhausting sweeping and struggling?
Ramu: Wait, wait—you're quoting our Guru now? He said "when something is accomplished in its natural order, that which is contrary gets eliminated." Don't you understand what he meant? He wasn't talking about sitting around waiting for understanding to magically appear! He meant that when you do the RIGHT practice in the RIGHT sequence, the obstacles naturally fall away. First you clean the step, then you can invite the guest. First discipline, then spontaneity. That's the natural order! You've completely twisted his teaching!
Somu: No, no, Ramu—you've made it mechanical! The Guru meant that when you switch on the light of understanding, the darkness of ignorance is naturally eliminated. You don't have to fight each shadow individually. One act of true seeing—properly accomplished—and all the contrary confusions just dissolve!
Ramu: But Somu, who flips the light switch? You do! And before you can flip it, someone had to install the wiring, someone had to ensure the electricity connection is proper. This is exactly my point—preparation is essential. You can't just sit around waiting for enlightenment to happen. You have to DO the work.
Somu: There you go again with this "I have to DO"! This is precisely the problem. When you're constantly doing, doing, doing—cleaning, practicing, perfecting—you're actually strengthening the sense of being the doer. True wisdom is recognizing that things happen naturally. The sun rises without effort, the flowers bloom without struggle. When understanding dawns, right action flows automatically.
Ramu: But the sun rises because of cosmic laws, the flowers bloom because the gardener watered them! Nothing happens without cause and effort. If I don't sweep my step, it stays dirty. If I don't meditate, my mind stays turbulent. This is simple cause and effect. Your "natural flow" is just laziness dressed up as philosophy.
Somu: And your discipline is just ego dressed up as spirituality! "I am doing this practice, I am making progress, I am becoming pure"—it's all I, I, I. Can't you see? The very effort to purify yourself assumes there's a separate self that needs purifying. When you truly understand, you realize there's nothing to do and no one to do it.
Ramu: So according to you, I should just stop everything? Stop my morning routine, let my house fall into chaos, abandon all practice? How is that going to lead to anything except degradation?
Somu: I'm not saying abandon everything mindlessly. I'm saying act from understanding, not from compulsion. When you see that light removes darkness, you don't worship the light switch—you simply use it naturally. Your problem is you're in love with the struggle itself.
Ramu: And your problem is you're so busy being clever with your analogies that you never actually DO anything! Philosophy doesn't sweep floors. Understanding doesn't prepare meals. Someone has to ACT.
* * *
(A moment of heavy silence falls between them. They've been circling this argument for months, neither budging. Both are frustrated, both convinced they're right.)
* * *
The Master (who has been sitting quietly in the corner): My dear friends, you've both presented your cases eloquently. But may I point something out?
Ramu & Somu (turning together): Please, Master.
Master: In this entire passionate discussion—and I've been listening carefully—there is one thing that remains absolutely consistent in both of your positions. Do you see it?
Somu: That... action and understanding are both necessary?
Master: No. Listen more carefully. Ramu says "I must sweep, I must practice, I must discipline myself." Somu says "I understand, I see clearly, I don't need to struggle."
(pauses)
The spotlight, the perspective from which both of you speak—it's the same. Both of you are convinced that YOU are doing it. Whether you're doing rigorous practice or doing effortless understanding, the doer is firmly in place. And as long as that doer is there, you are bound—regardless of whether the outcome is a clean step or a philosophical insight.
Ramu (defensive): But Master, if I don't do the practice, who will?
Master: Beautiful question. Stay with it. Really stay with it. But let me ask you both something else first—when a thought arises in your mind right now, did YOU create it? Did you sit down and manufacture it?
Somu: Well... no, thoughts just arise...
Master: And when your hand reaches for a glass of water, do you consciously command each muscle, each nerve fiber?
Ramu: No, it just... happens.
Master: So then, what if—just as an experiment, just to entertain the idea—what if you relinquished this sense of doership? Not abandoned action, but released the claim that "I am the one doing this."
Somu: But then who is acting?
Master: Ah! Now you're asking the right question. Let me put it this way: imagine you're an actor in a play. When you're deeply immersed in your role, you deliver your dialogues passionately—sometimes as the disciplined monk, sometimes as the wise philosopher. But what if, even while delivering these lines perfectly, you began to notice something?
Ramu: Notice what?
Master: That the dialogues—both the external ones you speak and the internal ones you think—are not being authored by you in this moment. You are a role, yes, but also a spectator. The words arise, the character speaks, and there's an awareness watching it all unfold.
Somu: Like... we're reading lines written by a director?
Master: Yes! At first, it feels that way. You maintain your character—Ramu still sweeps his step, Somu still has his insights—but now there's a subtle loosening. You're playing your part, but you're also watching the play. Every dialogue, every action, every thought—you begin to see them as movements in a grand performance.
Ramu: So we're just puppets? That sounds even more binding!
Master: Patience, my friend. This is just the beginning. When this perspective strengthens—when you consistently see yourself as both actor and spectator—something remarkable happens. You begin to see the Director everywhere.
Somu: Everywhere? What do you mean?
Master: In the sunrise that happens without your effort. In the breath that breathes you. In the thought that thinks itself. In Ramu's hands that sweep before Ramu decides to sweep. In Somu's insights that arise before Somu claims them. The Director is the intelligence that grows your fingernails, that heals your wounds, that dreams your dreams.
You see, when you're caught in doership, you think you're the author of a few tiny actions in a vast unconscious universe. But when doership relaxes, you begin to recognize the Author in everything—including what you thought were "your" actions.
Ramu (slowly): So... my morning practice...
Master: Still happens. But now Ramu watches Ramu practicing. The practice becomes more effortless, more joyful, because there's no "you" struggling to perfect "yourself." There's just practicing happening, watched by awareness.
Somu (thoughtfully): And my understanding...
Master: Still arises. But now Somu watches understanding blooming in Somu. There's no pride in it, no claim to it. Understanding happens, witnessed by awareness.
Somu (with sudden recognition): Master... wait. Is this... is this what the scriptures mean by ceaseless meditation? When they speak of unbroken awareness, the natural state—they're pointing to this, aren't they? Not sitting in a cave with closed eyes for hours, but this witnessing that continues whether Somu is talking, walking, or sleeping? And when they speak of seeing God everywhere, in all things—is it this? Seeing the Director's hand in every movement, every breath, every unfolding moment?
Master (eyes twinkling): Ah, Somu! Now you're beginning to see. Yes, when the witnessing becomes continuous, when there's always this awareness watching the play unfold—that is the meditation that never ends. Not because "you" are meditating constantly, but because awareness itself is always present, and you've simply stopped overlooking it.
Both: And then?
Master (smiles): And then... comes the final recognition. When you see the Director in everything, when every moment is clearly orchestrated by this vast Intelligence—the sunrises, the thoughts, the actions, the very seeking itself—a strange question bubbles up.
Ramu: What question?
Master: "Who is this Director?" And when you look deeply, when you look with the same awareness that's been watching the play all along—you find the most astounding thing.
Somu: Which is?
Master: There is no external Director. There never was. The separation between actor and Author, between character and Creator, between you and the Cosmic Intelligence—it was always imaginary. You yourself wrote this entire play, cast yourself in this role, created these debates, these struggles, these insights—all of it—to entertain yourself, to experience yourself, to wake up to yourself.
Ramu & Somu (in stunned silence)
Ramu (hesitantly): But Master... that last step seems like a huge jump. From watching a Director to realizing I AM the Director? That's...
Master: Yes, yes, I know—I jumped the gun a bit there! (laughs) You're right to question. Let me add some steps, because that leap is indeed too vast without preparation.
Somu: Please, Master. What comes between seeing the Director and realizing we ARE the Director?
Master: Ah, good. So let's say you've reached the stage where you clearly see the Director in everything—where life is obviously being lived through you, not by you. What typically happens next is a deep relaxation, a profound trust. You stop trying to control outcomes because you see that control was always illusory.
Ramu: That sounds peaceful, but also a bit... passive?
Master: Not at all! In fact, action becomes more spontaneous, more appropriate, more creative—because the anxiety of the separate doer is gone. But here's what's important: in this stage, there's still a subtle duality. There's "you" here, and there's the "Director" there. You're trusting the Director, surrendering to the Director, communing with the Director.
Somu: And that duality needs to collapse?
Master: It will collapse, naturally, but not through effort. What happens is this: as you live more and more from this surrendered state, you start noticing something peculiar. The Director seems to know you intimately. The Director knows exactly what you need, exactly when you need it. The Director's timing is impeccable, the Director's intelligence is perfectly tailored to your awakening.
Ramu: Well, yes, if the Director is God or the Cosmic Intelligence, wouldn't that make sense?
Master: It would, except... the intimacy is too perfect. It's as if the Director isn't just watching over you—the Director IS you, knows you from the inside, because there's no inside and outside. It's like... imagine watching a movie you don't remember making. At first, you're amazed at the story. Then you notice the characters feel strangely familiar. Then you recognize locations from your life. Then suddenly you see yourself on screen...
Somu: And you realize you filmed it yourself but forgot!
Master: Exactly! So there's an intermediate stage—let's call it the stage of intimate communion. You're in constant dialogue with the Director, and the dialogue is so fluid, so immediate, so perfectly responsive, that the boundary between prayer and answer, between question and response, between you and the Divine starts to blur.
Ramu: That's still not the final recognition though?
Master: No, because there's still a subtle "you" in communion with "That." The final shift happens when even this subtle separation is seen through. And this usually comes not through more practice or more understanding, but through grace—a sudden recognition that the entire framework of "me watching the Director" was itself part of the play.
Somu: How does that recognition happen?
Master: It's different for everyone. Sometimes it's triggered by a simple moment—you're watching the sunset, and suddenly you realize you're not separate from the watching. The sunset, the seeing, the seer—all arising in one seamless awareness. Or you're in deep meditation, and you notice that the awareness watching the thoughts is itself made of the same "substance" as the thoughts. Or you ask "Who am I?" deeply enough, and the question dissolves into the answer.
Ramu: But Master, even if I have such an experience, won't I just fall back into forgetting?
Master: Ah, that's the beauty of the gradual approach we've outlined! If you jump straight to "I am the Author of reality," the mind rebels, the ego inflates, or it becomes mere philosophy. But if you go step by step:
First: Notice you're doing everything—this is where you both are now.
Second: Entertain the possibility that you're not the doer, just playing a role and watching it.
Third: Recognize the Director in all things, surrender to that Intelligence.
Fourth: Experience intimate communion where the Director and you are in constant, seamless dialogue.
Fifth: Recognize that this dialogue is happening within a single awareness, and the separation was always imaginary.
Somu: Each stage naturally leading to the next...
Master: Exactly! (winks at Somu) See, your insight was correct, just applied at the wrong level.
Ramu: And each stage requiring practice and discipline to stabilize...
Master (winks at Ramu): And your insight was also correct, just applied with too much personal effort!
Both (laughing): So we were both right and both wrong!
Master: Welcome to the path, my friends. The arguing can stop now. Ramu, keep sweeping your step—but watch who sweeps. Somu, keep recognizing the natural flow—but notice who recognizes. And both of you, be patient. The play is unfolding exactly as it should.
Ramu: Master, one more question.
Master: Yes?
Ramu: If this is all a play we wrote to entertain ourselves, why did we write in so much suffering, so much confusion?
Master (smiling deeply): Because, my dear Ramu, what kind of play would it be if the protagonist knew the ending from the beginning? What kind of game would it be if there was never any challenge, never any forgetting, never any seeking? The suffering, the confusion, the debates between you and Somu—these are not mistakes in the script. They are the script. And the recognition that dawns at the end is sweeter precisely because of the darkness that came before.
Remember: even the light switch that Somu spoke of—it only makes sense because there was darkness to dispel.
Somu (quietly): And the cleaning that Ramu spoke of—it only has meaning because there was dust to remove.
Master: Now you're both seeing it. The whole play—with all its contradictions, its struggles, its different paths and perspectives—is perfect exactly as it is. Because it was designed by perfect intelligence for the purpose of rediscovering that very intelligence.
Ramu & Somu (bowing): Thank you, Master.
Master: Don't thank me. Thank the Director. Or rather... (chuckles)... thank yourself for writing such an interesting character as "the Master" into your play!
* * *

Epilogue

Ramu continued his daily practices, but with a lightness he'd never known. The step got swept, but he found himself smiling at the absurdity of "Ramu" taking credit for the sweeping.

Somu continued having his insights, but he noticed they arose from nowhere, stayed for a while, and dissolved back into nowhere—like waves in an ocean.

And sometimes, on quiet evenings, they would sit together in silence, two characters in a vast play, watching the Director direct, until even the watching dissolved into what was being watched.

A play written by THE DIRECTOR, via an actor who forgot they were playing a role.
