The Undifferentiable Absolute

A Journey Through Mathematics and Non-Duality
✦

Let's start with something simple. When mathematicians talk about differentiating a function, they're really asking: if we zoom in close enough at any point on this curve, does it look like a straight line? Can we find its slope, its rate of change, its tangent? Think of it like this: imagine we're driving on a hilly road. At any moment, we can ask "how steep is the road right here, right now?" If we can answer that question everywhere along the road, the road is differentiable. A smooth curve, a gentle hill, the path of a thrown ball, these are all differentiable. We can break them down, analyze them piece by piece, find the slope at each point. Differentiation is the mathematical way of taking something whole and examining it locally, point by point, asking "what's happening right here?"

Now here's where it gets interesting. There's this marvelous function in mathematics called the Weierstrass function. It's continuous, meaning we can draw it without lifting our pencil, yet it has no derivative anywhere. No matter how far we zoom in, hoping to find a smooth slope, a tangent line, some way to break it down into understandable pieces, we find only more wrinkles, more oscillations, more of the same irreducible complexity. The mathematicians tried to differentiate it, to analyze it point by point, and the function simply said "no." Not because it's broken or incomplete, but because it is what it is, whole and entire, refusing to be taken apart. Now here's the thing: when Ramana Maharshi kept asking "Who am I?" or when Advaita speaks of Brahman as that which cannot be described, they're pointing to exactly this kind of situation. The moment we ask the question, we've already created a false division. We've assumed there's a questioner separate from what's being questioned, an analyzer separate from what's being analyzed. We're trying to differentiate the non-differentiable. The function doesn't fail because it's too complex, and consciousness doesn't evade description because it's mysterious. They both simply are what they are, without parts, without separation, One without a second.

The Weierstrass Function: Continuous but Nowhere Differentiable

In Mathematics

The Weierstrass function resists differentiation because of infinite complexity at every scale. You cannot break it into parts.

In Advaita

Brahman resists description because of absolute simplicity and non-duality. There are no parts to distinguish.

✦

So when someone asks "Why did consciousness begin to move? Why did the One start dancing?" they're making the same delightful mistake. The question sneaks in all sorts of assumptions: that there was a before and after, that consciousness is one thing and movement is another, that there's a cause here and an effect there. It's like asking "What's one divided by zero?" The question isn't deep, it's malformed. There is no "why" because there is no second thing. The dance is not something consciousness does, the dance IS consciousness. Asking why is trying to split what cannot be split, differentiate what has no derivative. The Weierstrass function doesn't refuse to be differentiated out of stubbornness, it simply has no slopes to offer. Similarly, the Absolute doesn't hide the answer to "why", there simply is no duality required for the question to make sense.

"The dance is not something consciousness does; the dance IS consciousness."

✦

Now, we're using mathematics to point at something, and we must understand that this pointing is a kind of game, a finger gesturing toward the moon. The Weierstrass function isn't Brahman. One is infinitely complex in its roughness, the other is infinitely simple in its wholeness. But both share this quality: they cannot be grasped by the usual methods of taking things apart, of subject examining object, of questioner seeking answers. The beautiful irony is that by showing ourselves where mathematics breaks down, where the tools of analysis fail completely, we create a little opening. We feel the frustration of trying to differentiate what cannot be differentiated, and perhaps in that frustration, the questioner begins to dissolve. Not because we found the answer, but because we realized we were never separate from what we sought. The question falls away, and what remains is simply what always was: whole, complete, One without a second.

And that's the secret of all these teachings. They're not giving us information to add to our collection of knowledge. They're showing us where our very method of knowing breaks down completely. Ramana Maharshi's "Who am I?" exhausts itself. The mathematical analogy exhausts itself. The words being used right now will exhaust themselves. They're meant to. They're fingers pointing at the moon, and once we've looked, once we've seen, we don't need the finger anymore. We might even laugh at how seriously we took it, how we tried to grasp the ungraspable, differentiate the undifferentiable, separate what was never divided. The whole cosmic drama, the apparent movement from One to many, isn't a problem to be solved but a dance to be recognized as the dancer itself. And we, asking these questions, are not separate from that dance. We never were.
